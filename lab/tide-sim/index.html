In plain words

An ensemble

The tide is a piece played by many instruments. Each keeps its own beat: the moon’s big drum (M2) every 12 h 25 min, the sun’s snare (S2) every 12 h exactly, and others once a day (K1, O1) or once a year (Sa).

The score

Harmonic constants are a sea’s score. Moon and sun are the same everywhere, but the seabed and coastline set how loud (amplitude) and how late (phase) each instrument plays. Incheon’s big drum is huge (2.85 m); Venice’s is quiet. Write the score once and the sea can be replayed at any time.

The sum

The water level is every instrument added at that moment. Do it for each of the 525,600 minutes of a year and nothing is missed. Below, each arrow is one instrument; the tip of the chain is the water level.

Spring and neap

Drums in step play loud; out of step, soft. Their beats differ by 25 minutes, so about every 15 days they strike together and the range grows (spring), then drift apart and it shrinks (neap).

The diagram below tells the same story as a formula; the calculation follows it exactly.

How it works — water level from harmonic constants

M2
The moon’s twice-daily wave. 12 h 25 min. The largest.
S2
The sun’s twice-daily wave. Exactly 12 h.
N2
The moon drawing nearer and further. 12 h 40 min. Makes one spring tide a month larger.
K2
Moon and sun leaving the equator. 11 h 58 min.
K1 · O1 · P1 · Q1
Once-a-day waves — 23 h 56 · 25 h 49 · 24 h 04 · 26 h 52. They make the two high waters unequal.
Sa · Ssa
Yearly and half-yearly. Mean water rises and falls with the seasons.
M4 · MS4 · M6
Short waves born in shallow water (6 · 6.1 · 4.1 h). Make the flood and the ebb run at different speeds.
L2 · MU2 · NU2 …
Small waves from wobbles in the moon’s orbit. Mont-Saint-Michel, Tonkin and Venice use 40–44 of them.
  1. Split into waves. The tide is the sum of many waves raised by the pull of moon and sun, each with a period fixed by astronomy: M2 (moon) every 12 h 25 min, S2 (sun) every 12 h.
  2. Write down size and lag. Each wave’s size (H) and lag (g) in that sea.
  3. Find one moment’s height. Each wave is an arrow on the left. Its length is the size; it turns once per period. Its vertical part is what that wave adds right now.
  4. Add them up. Chain the arrows tip to tail; the last tip is the water level above mean sea level. The curve on the right is the path of that tip.
h(t) = Z₀ + Σ f·H·cos(ωt + V₀ + u − g) Z₀ mean sea level · H size · ω beat (fixed angular speed of each wave) · g local lag · V₀ astronomical angle at the start · f, u corrections for the 18.6-year cycle of the moon’s orbit

    In plain words — what the record is, and why

    Gauge posts

    Picture a post marked every 5 cm on the mudflat. In Incheon that is 196 posts, from the lowest reach of water (−0.20 m) to just above the highest (+9.55 m). Higher up, water never comes. One post is one line of the record.

    A diary

    Every post keeps a diary, every minute for a year. “Under water, or dry?” A year is 525,600 minutes, so each post writes 525,600 entries.

    Four questions

    The diary is summed up in four answers. How many days it floods (often), how long it stays above water (long), when it is dry by daylight (when), how fast the water rises (fast). Together they are the character of that height.

    In reverse

    Design reads the summary backwards. Set the answer first — “where does ground flood half the year?” — then sweep the posts upward until it appears. That is step 04.

    Why it is built this way — five rules

    1. Group by height. Ground at one height sees the same water anywhere on the site (shown in step 02). Collected by height instead of position, 6,161 points become 196 lines in Incheon with nothing lost. Only low pockets cut off from the sea differ, and step 02’s “flooding height” corrects for them.
    2. Read three spans. Water changes on three beats: the day (two highs, two lows), the fortnight (spring and neap) and the year (seasons, the moon’s tilt). One span alone hides the other two, so the record counts in the units people live by.
    3. Sum up in four questions. Using a space needs not the water level itself but “when can I go down, and how long can I stay”. So the record keeps count (often), length (long), timing (when) and speed (fast). None stands in for another.
    4. Every 5 cm. One waterside step (0.5 m) divided by ten. Each step gets ten lines — enough to tell its top from its bottom. Finer than this changes nothing in the design.
    5. Every minute, for a year. Slack water near low tide lasts just over two hours, and daylight is counted in hours; a minute counts both without error. A year is the shortest span that holds spring, neap and every season.

    How it works — from one ground height to one line

    Measure the same water at another height and the four answers change like this.

    1. How it is measured. Fix a height and compare it with each of the year’s 525,600 minutes of water. Water above: a flooded minute. Below: a dry one. Counted by day, daylight and hour, that is one line of the record.
    2. Higher ground, less water. Only days whose high water clears the height flood it, so higher ground floods less often and for less time, and stays dry longer and more often by day.
    3. Fastest in the middle. Water pauses at high and low tide and moves fastest near mean sea level, so speed peaks there and falls away above and below.
    4. Three heights where answers flip. Above the highest high water, water never comes; below the lowest high water, it comes every day; above the highest low water, ground dries every day. These three split the ground into four.

      Four questions, five variables which variable reads which column

      Heights and widths into one form four layout decisions, thesis 5.1

      1. Make it round. The data says nothing about direction, so one section sets the whole form.
      2. Highest floor in the middle. Water rises from the edge, so people can always step back inward.
      3. Ring the steps by their widths. The plinth’s size also comes from the speed of the water.
      4. A neck to the land, an apron to the sea. Steps run landward into a neck; the lower floor runs seaward into an apron, never dug below the mudflat.

      Why this plinth

      Tides run for one year from 1 October 2025, in each sea’s local time. Incheon uses 10 constants (Byun, 2011) on chart datum; the other three use 40–44 TICON-4 constants (Hart-Davis et al., 2025) on lowest astronomical tide.

      Water on screen is recomputed in the browser every 10 minutes; the height records and day groups use the thesis system’s one-minute output.
